Level uses Numerov’s method, which has a long and distiguished history in solving the one-dimensional Schrodinger equation. As Duo is based on another method, names the sinc DVR, it is perhaps worthwhile to spend a few words comparing the different approaches.
The Numerov takes its name from the Russian astronomer Boris Numerov, who introduced it in the 1920s for astronomical calculations. Specifically, Numerov’s method is a convenient way to discretise second-order differential equations when the first-derivative term is not present, i.e. it is applicable to equations of the type y’’(x) = f(x, y). The Schrodinger equation can be written in this form, psi’’(x) = (2 m/hbar^2)[V(x)-E]psi(x)=f(x) psi(x).
By discreting the equation on a uniformely spaced grid with step h, the right-hand side becomes f(xi) psi(xi); for the left hand side we need a grid-approximation of the second derivative operator. The most staightforward choice is to use the simple three-point formula for the second derivative
psi’’(x)=(psi(x-h)+psi(x+h)-2psi(x))/h^2
This leads to the three-point propagatin expression , which is accurate to O(h^2).

Numerov’s method was considered by Hartree for atoms.
Later, other authors such as Lowdin, Cooley have contributed to refinement of the shooting procedure. We’ll call this combination N/S.
One advantage of N/S is that it requires very little memory, which was probably a very important consideration in the early years. With N/S one only needs to store a handful of constants independently of the grid step. 
Another advantage is that each eigenvalue can be computed completely independently, and you can compute directly the nth eigenvalue.
Finally, the convergence is O(h^4), which is usually fast enough.
Numerov’s method is satisfactory in many cases, but it when high accuracy is required.
A practical limitation in accuracy is due to numerical round-off
